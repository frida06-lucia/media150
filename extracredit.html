Nancy Baker Cahill Talk







Throughout Nancy Baker Cahill’s artist talk, I was constantly in awe of the kind of work that can be created and contributed to with modern technology. Before this talk, I knew very little about how media and technology could be integrated into the art world in such immersive and meaningful ways. What stood out most to me was her ability to combine physical experience with digital media through augmented reality, projection mapping, sound, and sensory interaction. Her projects demonstrated that art is much more than just sculpture and paint on a canvas and is no longer limited to something we only look at, but something we can experience through multiple senses and perspectives. One of the most interesting ideas Cahill discussed was the way AR/Augmented Reality exists as both invisible and visible at the same time. Without technology, these works cannot fully be seen, this fact only elevates the experience of this work as they still exist in the environment and influence the way people interact with space. This challenged my understanding of public art because it creates a form of collaboration between the artist, the audience, and technology itself. This to me was somewhat reminiscent of the performance pieces we focused on with “Cut Piece” by Yoko Ono, in which the audience is required to participate and blur the boundaries of the artists and audience, as the audience participates, they become the artist as well.


The participatory audience aspect of AR art is seen most notably in “CENTO” which is a global participatory art project that combines the immersive video and AR to imagine the future hybrid creature designed to survive the sixth extinction and environmental collapse. The creature is made up of human, animal, microbial, marine, fungal, and machine parts. This project explores themes of synthetic biology, survival, and collective responsibility in the face of ecological crisis. One of the most important and effective aspects of CENTO is its interactive AR component, where viewers around the world can participate in the creature’s survival by adding virtual feathers through an app. Each feather gives the creature new properties, allowing it to evolve and grow stronger in real time through public participation. The idea of standing still while the artwork circles around you creates a completely different relationship between the audience and the piece. These same ecological themes continue throughout most of Nancy Baker Cahill’s work and are especially visible in Carbon 1, where she explores ecological collapse and noise created by humans, machines, and technology. This piece layered together images of nature, industrial plants, animal skins, fire, and AI-generated botanical forms to create a fractured and unstable environment. As the film progresses, pixelated digital noise increasingly distorts the landscape until the world we know is completely erased. The fragmented visuals and chaotic feeling in Carbon 1 and Aftermath reminded me of the animated documentary Ryan where the characters physically wear their emotional and psychological trauma on their fragmented bodies, with transparent faces and distorted features representing inner damage. Similarly, Cahill uses distortion, fragmentation, and deconstruction not just as a visual style, but as a way to communicate emotional, environmental, and societal collapse. Both works force viewers to confront uncomfortable realities that might otherwise be ignored.


Nancy Baker Cahill’s talk expanded my understanding of what contemporary art can be. Her projects combine technology like AI and AR proving environmental awareness, collaboration, and sensory experience in ways that challenge traditional ideas of drawing and public art. I left the talk thinking more deeply about how digital tools like AR and VR can create effecting art pieces about environmentalism and participatory projects. Another exhibition that deepened my understanding of Cahill’s work was CORPUS, which imagines a future where humans, machines, microorganisms, and nature become increasingly interconnected. The project centers around a massive AR “symborg” figure that combines metallic, glowing, and organic elements into a constantly shifting body. What interested me most was the way the figure appears both technological and biological at the same time, almost dissolving the boundary between human life and machine systems. Parts of the body appear transparent, emphasizing instability and fluidity rather than presenting the body as fixed or permanent. Cahill’s idea of the body as a constantly changing process instead of a stable object reflects many of the same themes present throughout her other work, especially ideas about ecological dependence, adaptation, and hybrid existence. The sound design in CORPUS also contributes heavily to the immersive experience. Cahill combines recordings of heartbeats from humans, fetuses, whales, and even black holes colliding in space alongside AI-generated sound. This blending of natural, cosmic, and artificial audio creates an atmosphere where distinctions between living organisms and technological systems begin to collapse. I found this especially interesting because the work suggests that consciousness and feeling are not exclusive to humans alone. Instead, Cahill presents life as something interconnected across biological, environmental, and technological systems. This perspective challenges the traditional human-centered view of the world and instead positions humanity as only one small part within a much larger network of life.


I was also struck by the scale of CORPUS and the way it physically dwarfs viewers. By making the AR figure enormous in comparison to the audience, Cahill reverses the assumption that humans dominate or control the world around them. The viewer becomes physically smaller and less powerful, which reinforces the ecological themes present throughout the project. This inversion of scale forces audiences to reconsider their relationship to technology, nature, and even their own bodies. Rather than placing humans at the center of existence, the work emphasizes interdependence and vulnerability. The towering figure almost feels like a vision of a future species created through environmental collapse, technological advancement, and biological adaptation. The Mushroom Cloud Project similarly explores environmental destruction but through a different visual language. Cahill creates an AR mushroom cloud rising over the ocean, immediately invoking associations with war, catastrophe, and human-made disaster. However, the cloud eventually transforms into a mycelial network, connecting destruction with the possibility of regeneration and survival. I found this transformation particularly meaningful because it balances despair with hope. Instead of presenting environmental collapse as purely apocalyptic, Cahill imagines the possibility of rebuilding through collective care and interconnectedness. The use of fungal imagery and mycelium becomes symbolic of decentralized support systems where survival depends on cooperation rather than domination.


What makes the Mushroom Cloud Project especially compelling is that the work exists directly within vulnerable coastal environments affected by climate change and rising sea levels. By placing the AR cloud over the Pacific Ocean, Cahill turns the landscape itself into part of the artwork. This creates a direct confrontation between viewers and the environmental realities being referenced. The project also highlights how invisible systems connect people globally, much like mycelial networks beneath the earth. Cahill’s emphasis on participation, accountability, and communal responsibility transforms the artwork into more than just a visual experience. It becomes a call for audiences to rethink how communities respond to environmental crises and how technology might be used not only to document collapse, but to imagine new forms of survival and collective care. In conclusion, Nancy Baker Cahill’s work demonstrates how contemporary art can use technology not only as a creative tool, but also as a way to explore environmental crisis, interconnectedness, and the future of human existence. Through projects like CENTO, Carbon 1, CORPUS, and the Mushroom Cloud Project, Cahill challenges audiences to rethink their relationship to nature, technology, and one another. Her use of AR, immersive sound, projection mapping, and participatory experiences transforms viewers from passive observers into active participants within the artwork itself. What I found most impactful was the way her projects balance feelings of fear, instability, and collapse with ideas of collaboration, adaptation, and collective care. Rather than presenting technology as something separate from humanity or nature, Cahill shows how deeply interconnected these systems already are. Her work ultimately expanded my understanding of contemporary art by proving that digital media can create emotionally powerful experiences that encourage audiences to reflect on ecological responsibility, public participation, and the uncertain future of our world.
