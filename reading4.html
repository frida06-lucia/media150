Nuclear War: The Rising Rish and How We Stop It


The New York Times article titled “Nuclear War and How To Stop It” is an extremely affecting piece. The blend of an interactive animated story told through multiple illustrations really showcases the dire need and attention this problem needs. The main color used in these articles is red, associating it with danger and fear. We see many shades of red change and shift throughout the story. We start off with just the facts of how we are at risk of nuclear war and how rising political tensions are only further exacerbating this. Then we move into the most affecting part of this article, the images of “what if”. The “what if” for this story is followed by a very effective illustration that is giving us the reality of what would happen if nuclear war were to happen and how we would all be effective. THis part of the article is very blunt, saying how we are either greatly injured or dead all together.


All wildlife and pets would also die and turn to ash. Any survivors would be too injured to help any one leaving us for dead or to suffer a slow and painful one. Moving into the visual of the nuclear arms race where it tracks what country and world power has nuclear power and how it grows and changes over time. The design decision to have most of the text scrolling past us and constantly moving as we move further into the article. This choice helps further the anxiety we feel as we continue reading this. Moving down towards the end this article does not leave us in a very optimistic place, it does not attempt to comfort us in any way. It instead leaves us on a very uncertain note, much like how this same situation is playing out now. The shift to the nuclear arms race visualization is also significant. After immersing the reader in the consequences, the article zooms out to show the systems that make those consequences possible. Tracking which countries possess nuclear weapons and how those arsenals have grown over time introduces a sense of inevitability and scale. It reminds the reader that this isn’t a distant or outdated threat—it’s an ongoing, measurable reality shaped by political decisions. Another key design choice is the scrolling experience itself. The movement of text and imagery creates a sense of momentum that the reader can’t easily interrupt. Unlike a static article, where you can pause or skim, this format encourages continuous engagement. The act of scrolling becomes almost complicit—you’re moving forward, deeper into the scenario, unable to fully detach. This contributes to the anxiety the piece generates, as the pacing is controlled in a way that mirrors escalation. Overall, the article succeeds because it combines factual reporting with emotional immersion. It doesn’t just inform, it forces the reader to confront the stakes in a way that’s difficult to ignore, using design, color, pacing, and narrative structure to make the abstract threat of nuclear war feel immediate and deeply personal.
